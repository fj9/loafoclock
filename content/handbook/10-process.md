## Step 1: Feed the starter {steps=feed}

Feed it so that it will be ripe when you want to mix. Work backwards from the mixing time using the table in [[starter#feeding-and-ratios|section 3.3]]. For example, a {{standardFeed}} feed (the app's standard) is ready in {{feedWindow 6}}. The app starts from a starter kept in the fridge, fed straight from the jar.

A **levain** is not a different thing from your starter: it is a portion of it, built up in a separate jar for the bake, so the main starter jar stays untouched. Many bakers build enough for the recipe plus a spoonful to keep going.

## Step 2: Autolyse {steps=autolyse}

Mix the flour and water only. Cover and rest for {{restMin}} to {{restMax}} minutes ({{restDefault}} is the usual). The starter is not in yet, so bulk fermentation has not started.

Why bother:

- The flour fully absorbs the water, so the dough is not left with dry patches.
- Gluten starts to form on its own, with no kneading, so the dough becomes smoother and more stretchy, and is easier to handle and fold.
- Enzymes in the flour get a head start. They loosen the dough and tend to help the crumb and the crust colour.
- It costs you nothing but time: the rest is also when the starter finishes reaching its peak.

Some flours respond to it better than others, so bake with it and without it and note the difference.

**You can skip it in a pinch.** If you do not have the spare hour, mix the flour, water and starter together in one go and start bulk straight away. The dough will feel stickier and slacker at first, and the folds do a bit more of the work, but the flour keeps absorbing water during the rest that follows, and the loaf will still be good. You lose a little smoothness and some of the open crumb. The [[example-after-work|after work example]] skips it for exactly this reason. The app plans an autolyse, so to skip it, ignore the autolyse step and mix everything when the "Add the starter" step is due.

The app adds the starter after the rest, then rests the dough again. That second rest is called the **fermentolyse** (it is the next step). Salt goes in after that, because it tightens dough and slows enzyme action.

## Bulk fermentation (the first rise) {#bulk steps=starterIn,preShape}

This is the longest and most important stage of the process. It builds flavour, structure and gas. Steps 3 to 6 all happen inside it, so it is worth knowing what it is before you start step 3.

Bulk fermentation is a period, not a step of its own. It runs through {{phase bulk}}: it starts when the starter goes in and ends at the pre-shape, so the salt rest and the folds happen inside it.

The dough will not look like much for the first hour. This is normal. Resist the urge to poke it every ten minutes.

Bulk time by dough temperature. Measure the dough with a probe just after the starter is mixed in, then look up the closest temperature. Bulk is counted from the moment the starter goes in.

{{bulkTable}}

Treat the table as a starting guess. It is written for a ripe, active starter and a dough at that temperature, in the app's recipe (about {{starterPercent}}% starter and {{hydration}}% hydration). Less starter, or a sleepy one, means longer; more starter means shorter. Your kitchen, flour and starter will all nudge the answer, and learning how they do it is most of the craft. Go by the cues first and the clock second, and start checking at the shorter time in the range.

{{bulkTooCool}} {{bulkTooWarm}}

To warm a dough that is too cool, mix with warm water and keep it somewhere warm, such as the oven with only the light on ([[levers#lever-2-dough-temperature-how-fast-bulk-goes|section 8, lever 2]]).

Judge by the dough, not the clock:

- Volume: the table gives the target rise for your dough temperature. A warm dough needs less rise than a cool one. Mark the start level on a straight-sided tub, or use a small straight-sided jar of dough (an aliquot jar).
- Surface: domed, slightly glossy, with bubbles visible at the edges or surface.
- Edges: the dough pulls away from the container cleanly.
- Feel: airy, jiggly like jelly, and more cohesive than at the start.
- Wobble test: gently shake the tub; if the whole mass jiggles, it is likely ready.

Underfermented: little rise, dense, tight, tears when shaped. Gives a tight, gummy crumb with small holes and often a pale crust.

Overfermented: slack, sticky, collapses when shaped, smells sharply sour. Gives flat loaves, thin pale crust and gummy sticky crumb.

## Step 3: Add the starter {steps=starterIn}

1. Add the active starter to the rested flour and water.
2. Use wet hands to squeeze, fold and pinch the dough until the starter is fully mixed in. This takes 2 to 5 minutes by hand; a stand mixer takes a couple of minutes on low. The dough will look shaggy and rough. That is fine.
3. Rest the dough for {{restMin}} to {{restMax}} minutes ({{restDefault}} is the usual). This rest is the **fermentolyse**: the starter and dough get to know each other before the salt goes in. Bulk fermentation starts when the starter goes in, so note the time and measure the dough temperature now (see [[process#bulk|bulk fermentation]] above).

## Step 4: Add the salt {steps=salt}

1. Add the salt, and the honey and olive oil if you are using them.
2. Work them in until evenly spread. A teaspoon or two of warm water helps dissolve the salt, and is too little to change the dough.
3. Rest the dough for {{saltRest}} minutes. Bulk is already under way, so the salt rest is part of it.

## Step 5: Folds {steps=fold1,fold2,fold3,fold4}

The folds build the dough's strength. You want gluten strong enough to hold gas but not overworked. There are several approaches:

| Method | How | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Stretch and folds | Wet hand, lift one side of the dough high, fold over the centre. Turn the bowl a quarter and repeat 4 times. Do 3–6 sets, spaced 20–45 minutes apart in the first half of bulk. | The most popular home method; gentle and low effort |
| Coil folds | Lift the centre of the dough and let it fold underneath itself in a coil. | Wetter doughs; very gentle |
| Slap and fold / kneading | Work the dough on the bench for 5–10 minutes. | Faster strength building; stiffer or enriched doughs |
| Stand mixer | A few minutes at low speed, then rest. | Large batches, wet doughs |
| No-knead | Long rest with few or no folds. | Very wet doughs and lazy mornings |

In the app: {{foldCount}} sets of stretch and folds, {{foldGap}} minutes apart, the first one {{saltRest}} minutes after the salt goes in, which is inside bulk. Add inclusions (the extras you mix in) once the dough has some strength. Dry ones such as nuts and dried fruit go in at the second or third fold, and wet or sticky ones such as olives and cheese go in at the last fold (see [[variations|section 12]]).

A good rule: do the folds early in bulk while the dough is still slack, then leave it alone for the last half so the gas can build up undisturbed.

To check the gluten, you can try the **windowpane test** (optional). Wet your fingers, pull off a small piece of dough and stretch it gently. If it stretches thin enough to let light through without tearing, the gluten is well developed.

## Step 6: Pre-shape {steps=preShape}

1. Tip the dough gently onto a lightly floured (or unfloured) bench.
2. Divide if making more than one loaf.
3. Gather each piece into a loose round, using a scraper to drag it and build surface tension.
4. Rest uncovered or covered for {{benchRest}} minutes. The dough relaxes so the final shape is easier.

## Step 7: Final shape {steps=finalShape}

Aim for a taut surface and a closed seam without tearing the skin.

Boule (round): pull the edges to the centre in a rough envelope, flip, and drag toward you on the bench to tighten.

Batard (oval): fold the top down, fold the sides in like a letter, roll up from the top, seal the seam.

Place seam-side up in a well-floured banneton (rice flour works well because it does not stick), or seam-side down in a lined bowl.

## Step 8: Final proof {steps=fridge}

Two main options:

| Option | How | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Room temperature proof | covered, for the time in the alternative to this step, below | Milder flavour; quicker; easy to overproof |
| Fridge proof | {{fridgeMin}} to {{fridgeMax}} hours at about 3–5°C | More flavour, blistered crust, easier scoring, flexible timing |

Cover the banneton before it goes in the fridge, with a reusable shower cap, a large bag or a damp tea towel, so the dough does not dry out and form a skin.

The **poke test** is the classic readiness check. Flour a finger and press about 1 cm into the dough:

- Springs back immediately: underproofed, needs longer.
- Springs back slowly and leaves a slight dent: ready.
- Does not spring back and stays dented: fully proofed, bake now.

Cold dough is firmer, so the poke test is less reliable straight from the fridge. Judge by volume, wobble and a domed top as well.

## Step 8, alternative: Final proof without the fridge {#final-proof-no-fridge steps=fridge}

This is the alternative to the fridge proof in step 8. The house method is a fridge proof for the final proof only (see [[levers#lever-3-fridge-proof|section 8, lever 3]]). If you skip the fridge, proof the shaped dough covered at room temperature, then bake. The loaf is milder and the timing is less flexible.

Final proof is much shorter than bulk, so check early.

{{finalProofTable}}

Treat the table as a starting point for your own records. The times are for a ripe, active starter and shaped dough in a room at that temperature. For example, at 24°C allow {{finalProof 24}}, and start checking at the shorter time. Cue first, clock second: the dough looks puffy, and the poke test springs back slowly and leaves a slight dent. If it springs back at once, give it longer. If it does not spring back at all, bake now. Note how your own kitchen and starter compare, and adjust next time.

Turn the oven on while the dough finishes proofing, so the pot is hot when it is ready ({{preheat}} minutes).

## Step 9: Bake {steps=ovenOn,bake}

Score the dough, then bake it. See [[baking]] for details.

## Step 10: Cool and carve {steps=out,carve}

Cool on a wire rack for at least {{coolingLong}} minutes ({{coolingShort}} if you have no self-control). The app offers both, with {{coolingLong}} as the default. The crumb is still setting and steaming inside; cutting early gives a gummy texture and compresses the loaf.

You should hear the crust crackle as it cools. This is called "singing".
