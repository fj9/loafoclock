Almost every decision in sourdough comes down to these three. Once you understand them, you can bend the process around your schedule.

## Lever 1: Feed ratio (when your starter peaks) {steps=feed}

- Small feed (1:1:1): peaks sooner, in {{feedWindow 1}}.
- Large feed (1:10:10): peaks later, in {{feedWindow 10}}.
- Use this to time the starter to be ripe when you are ready, whether that is after work or the next morning.

## Lever 2: Dough temperature (how fast bulk goes)

- Warmer dough ferments faster and cooler dough ferments more slowly. The chart in [[process#bulk|section 7, bulk fermentation]] gives the bulk time for each dough temperature.
- The chart covers dough from {{bulkCoolest}} to {{bulkWarmest}}°C. Outside that, go by the cues rather than the clock.
- Warm dough (26 to 28°C): faster, milder, more yeasty-sweet.
- Cool dough (20 to 22°C): slower, tends to be more sour, more flavour development.
- No thermometer? Pick the dough temperature closest to your kitchen. It is a fair guess, but a probe is better.
- Control it with the temperature of the water you mix with and where you leave the dough.

Desired Dough Temperature (DDT) formula:

> Water temp = (DDT × 4) − flour temp − room temp − starter temp − friction factor

- Typical DDT: 24 to 26°C.
- Friction factor: about 0 for hand mixing, roughly 5 to 8°C for a stand mixer.

Example: DDT 25°C, flour 22°C, room 22°C, starter 24°C, hand mixed → 100 − 22 − 22 − 24 − 0 = 32°C water.

(Work in one unit throughout, and treat the answer as a starting point. Measure the dough after mixing and adjust next time.)

Quick ways to adjust:

- Too cold: warm water, a proving box, near a radiator (not on it), or a bowl of warm water beside the dough in a cooler box. Your oven can help too (see below).
- Too warm: cool water, a cooler room, the fridge for part of bulk.

Using the oven as a warm, draught-free box:

- Oven off, light on: the bulb warms the closed oven gently and keeps draughts away. Some LED bulbs give off little heat, so put a thermometer inside and check after 30 minutes. Every oven differs.
- Oven off, with a tray of boiling water: stand a sheet pan of boiling water on the bottom shelf and the dough on a shelf above. This is warmer and more humid.
- **Warning:** make sure the oven is switched off and tell everyone in the house that the dough is in there. Take care carrying a tray of boiling water, and take the dough out before you turn the oven on.

## Lever 3: Fridge proof (when the dough is ready) {#lever-3-fridge-proof steps=fridge}

- Fridge temperatures slow fermentation to a crawl but do not stop it; bacteria keep working, so acidity and flavour keep building.
- The app allows {{fridgeMin}} to {{fridgeMax}} hours, so a fridge proof fits around sleep or work.
- You can use the fridge during bulk (a cold bulk after a partial rise) or during final proof (after shaping). Final proof is more common and more predictable.
- Longer cold times (24 hours and more, up to {{fridgeMax}}) give more tang and deeper flavour.
- Very long fridge times, towards {{fridgeMax}} hours, are for the curious. The dough can overproof, so judge it by feel, note what happens and adjust. Finding where your starter and kitchen sit is part of the fun.
- Cold dough is firmer, so it is easier to handle, score and transfer.
- Cover the dough for the fridge proof (see [[process#step-8-final-proof|section 7, final proof]]).
- Bake straight from the fridge. No need to let it warm.
- To skip the fridge, proof at room temperature for a much shorter time instead ([[process#final-proof-no-fridge|Final proof without the fridge]]). The loaf is milder.

How the three levers interact: a smaller feed, a warmer room and a short fridge proof make for a quick, mild bake. A big feed, a cool room and a long fridge proof make for a slow, tangy, flexible bake. Mix and match to fit your day.
