## What it is and where to get one

A paste or batter of flour and water that has been fed repeatedly until a stable community of yeast and LAB lives in it. A healthy starter doubles (or more) in a predictable time after feeding, smells pleasantly sour, fruity or yeasty, and is full of bubbles.

You can get a starter in three ways:

- Borrow some. A friend, a neighbour or a local bakery that bakes sourdough will often give you a spoonful of an established starter. This is usually the quickest route. Ask what they feed it, and feed it the same way for a week or so before you change anything.
- Buy a mature starter. Some bakers and shops sell starters, fresh or dried. Follow the seller's instructions for waking it up. It will usually need a few feeds before it doubles reliably, so do not bake with it on the day it arrives.
- Make your own from flour and water. It costs almost nothing but takes about 7 to 14 days and some patience (see the next section).

Whichever route you take, the recipes here assume a starter that reliably doubles within about 4 to 8 hours of feeding (at roughly 24°C) for several days running, and smells good. Until yours does, keep feeding it and hold off baking, because a young starter makes a flat, dense loaf. Check a starter you are given or buy in the same way as your own ([[starter#starter-troubleshooting|starter troubleshooting]] and [[food-safety|food safety]]).

## Making one from scratch (about 7 to 14 days)

Day 1:

1. 50 g wholemeal (or rye) flour + 50 g lukewarm water in a clean jar. Stir well.
2. Cover loosely (a lid resting on top, not screwed down) and keep somewhere around 24 to 27°C.

Days 2 to 14:

1. Once a day, keep about 50 g of the mixture and discard the rest.
2. Add 50 g flour + 50 g water, stir, and cover loosely.
3. Switch to twice a day when it starts to rise reliably.
4. Mark the level with an elastic band to see growth.

What you may see

- Days 2 to 4: Early bubbles and a funky, sometimes unpleasant smell. This is normal and is often a different set of bacteria that will fade. Think of it as the starter's awkward teenage phase.
- A quiet patch: Activity often dips, sometimes with little rise. Keep feeding on schedule.
- Days 5 to 10: Rise returns, the smell turns pleasantly sour or fruity.

Ready when: it reliably doubles within about 4 to 8 hours of feeding (at roughly 24°C) for several days running, and smells good rather than sharp or like nail polish remover.

Tips:

- Using wholemeal or rye for the first week speeds things up because it carries more microbes and nutrients.
- A warmer spot (25 to 28°C) helps. A cold kitchen can stretch the process to three weeks.
- Do not give up early: the first few days of bubbles are not a mature starter.

## Feeding and ratios {steps=feed}

Feed ratios are written starter : flour : water by weight.

{{feedTable}}

A small feed such as 1:1:1 is fast, active and milder, and suits frequent feeding. A big feed such as 1:10:10 is slow and suits an overnight build. The app's standard feed is {{standardFeed}}, which fits a normal day. The app picks the feed for you from how long you want to wait.

The principle: the more fresh food relative to the old starter, the longer it takes to peak. A bigger feed gives you more time; a smaller feed is quicker. Warmth shortens all of these times; cold lengthens them.

These windows are for a kitchen of about 21 to 22°C. In a cooler kitchen they are longer: a {{standardFeed}} at 18°C takes a couple of hours longer. Peak times are approximate and vary with flour type, temperature and how strong your starter is. Learn your own starter's rhythm by marking its level and noting the time it peaks.

## Hydration of the starter

- 100% hydration (equal weights flour and water) is the most common and what most recipes assume.
- Stiff starter (50 to 60% hydration) ferments more slowly and tends to produce more acetic (sharper) acids.
- Liquid starter (150%+) ferments faster and favours lactic (milder) acidity.

## Maintenance

Room temperature (baking often): feed once or twice a day.

Fridge (baking occasionally):

- Feed, let it sit for an hour or two until it starts to rise.
- Store in the fridge in a loosely covered jar.
- Refresh about once a week (feed and return to the fridge), or longer if you accept a slower revival.
- To bake: take it out and feed it. The app's plan starts with that feed, using starter straight from the fridge. If it has been sitting a long time or looks sluggish, feed it once or twice first, over 12 to 24 hours, until it is doubling reliably again.

Dried backup: spread a thin layer of fed starter on parchment, let it dry fully, crumble, and keep in an airtight container. Rehydrate with water and flour when needed.

## Is it ready to use? (the "peak") {steps=feed,starterIn}

Use it when it is **ripe**, not past its best:

- Roughly doubled (or more) in volume
- Domed top, bubbly through the side of the jar
- Smells sweet, yeasty and mildly sour
- Texture is airy and bubbly when stirred

Past peak: the dome collapses or flattens, it smells sharply sour or of acetone, and it may have a liquid layer. It will still work but more slowly. Feed it and use it at the next peak.

**The float test** (dropping a spoonful in water) is unreliable. A starter can float and still be weak, and a ripe starter can sink if it has been stirred down. Trust volume and bubbles over the float test.

## Discard

When you feed, you remove most of the old starter to keep the quantity manageable and keep the culture healthy. This is the discard. It is safe to use, and it makes excellent pancakes, crackers, flatbreads, waffles and muffins ([[variations|section 13]]). It can also go into a bowl in the fridge until you have enough.

## Starter troubleshooting

| Symptom | Meaning | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Grey or dark liquid on top (hooch) | Hungry starter; alcohol by-product | Harmless. Stir it in (more sour) or pour it off, then feed. |
| Dark skin or crust on top | The surface has dried out | Harmless if there is no fuzz and no pink or orange. Stir it in or lift it off, then feed. |
| Sharp smell like nail polish remover | Very hungry | Feed more often or use a bigger feed. |
| Very little rise | Too cold, underfed or young | Warm it up, feed more often, use some wholemeal or rye. |
| Rises then collapses fast | Peaked and passed | Use it earlier or give a larger feed. |
| Fuzzy mould, any colour | Contaminated | Throw it all away and start again. |
| Pink or orange streaks | Contaminated | Throw it all away and start again. |
| Thin, runny and weak | Too much water or underfed | Feed regularly with slightly more flour than water. |

Hooch and a dark skin on top are fine. Mould and pink or orange streaks are not.
